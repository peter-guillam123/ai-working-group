The Fairford arrests: what we know, and what we don’t

Five men were held near a base used by US bombers, then bailed a day later. Here is what police have confirmed, what politicians have claimed, and why the press has to tread with care.

Based on sources checked up to 4pm BST, Tuesday 29 September 2026. The story is moving; this page is not updated live.

4 · The law

Why the press is holding back

Many readers assume the rules on reporting a case begin when someone is charged. In England and Wales they begin at arrest.

The Contempt of Court Act 1981 makes it an offence to publish anything that creates “a substantial risk” that a trial will be “seriously impeded or prejudiced”, whatever the publisher meant.L1 The rule bites once a case is “active”, and a case becomes active when someone is arrested without a warrant.L2

Bail does not switch it off. Since March 2018, a case in England and Wales stays active until the suspect is charged and tried, or is told there will be no prosecution.L3 So the Fairford case is active now. A publisher who breaks it faces an unlimited fine, and those responsible up to two years in jail.L1

This is not theory. In 2011 the Mirror and the Sun were found in contempt over their coverage of Christopher Jefferies, a man arrested over a murder in Bristol, bailed and never charged.L4

Step through the case

Choose a stage, or use the arrow keys. The yellow dot marks where the case stands today.

Arrest: the rules switch on

Armed officers arrested the five in the early hours of Sunday. By 2pm, police had arrested them again on suspicion of preparing an act of terrorism.P2

The explosives arrest. Police said “the Explosives Act” but not which law or section.P3 The most serious charge of this kind, under section 4 of the Explosive Substances Act 1883, covers making or holding an explosive substance where there is good reason to suspect it is not for a lawful purpose. The maximum sentence is life.L14

The terror arrest: Terrorism Act 2006, section 5. It is a crime to do anything to prepare for an act of terrorism, even without a set target. The maximum is life.L15 Where explosives are used, the law counts an act as terrorism without needing to show an aim to sway a government or scare the public.L16

Safe to report

  • The arrests, the offences, the ages and home areas police gave
  • What police and ministers said on the record
  • Wider debate about US bases and threats from states

Risky

  • Anything that assumes guilt
  • Names, photos, past convictions, character
  • Talk of what the evidence shows

Based on Attorney General and CPS guidance.L8L9 Section 5 of the 1981 act protects good-faith debate of public affairs, where any risk to a case is incidental.L5

Custody: about a day and a half

It depends on which power police use. Here, police said the men were held under the ordinary law, the Police and Criminal Evidence Act, and got more time in custody “until later today” on Monday.P4

Under that law, police can hold a suspect for 24 hours, 36 with a superintendent’s say-so, and up to 96 with a magistrate’s warrant.L10 The Terrorism Act 2000 has its own power: 48 hours, and up to 14 days with a judge’s warrant.L11 Some reports this week cited that 14-day limit, but it was not the power used here.

The choice matters for what came next. People held under the terrorism power cannot be given bail with conditions: they must be charged or let go.L12 The ordinary law allows bail with conditions, which is what happened.

Bail: free to go, not in the clear

At about 3.45pm on Monday police released all five on bail, with “stringent conditions on their movement and contact with others”. Assistant Commissioner Laurence Taylor said they “absolutely remain under investigation”.P5

Bail before charge is not a verdict either way. Police can only grant it if they still suspect the person and need to investigate further.L13 In general, conditions can cover where someone lives, whom they contact and whether they can travel. For some terror offences, breaking a travel condition is itself a crime.L19

The first period of bail lasts three months, and senior officers or a court can extend it. By our count, that first period runs to about the end of December.L13 Police can arrest the men again if new evidence emerges.L17

Trump said he would not have released them. On Tuesday, Labour’s deputy leader, Lucy Powell, said bail decisions are “operational decisions for the police”.1215

Contempt rules

Still on. Bail changes nothing.L3

Names

Not released. Police name people only in exceptional cases before charge, and the Supreme Court has said people under investigation can expect privacy until then.L6L7

Next: charge, or no further action

In terror cases the Crown Prosecution Service’s counter-terrorism division decides on charges, not the police.L18 Prosecutions under both laws need sign-off from the top: the Attorney General for the explosives offence, and the Director of Public Prosecutions for the terror offence, who needs the Attorney General’s permission if a foreign country is involved.L14L15

If a foreign power were shown to be behind it, the National Security Act 2023 could also apply, with its own offences such as sabotage.L21

If charged

Police normally name people when they charge them.L6 The contempt rules get stricter in practice as a trial nears.

If not

The case stops being active once each man is told he will not be prosecuted.L3

Court: reporting opens up

Once a case reaches court, the press can publish fair and accurate reports of what happens in open court, at the time it happens.L1 What stays off limits is anything a jury would not hear.

Reform may come. In November 2025 the Law Commission proposed that cases should become active at charge rather than arrest. It had looked again at the question after the Southport murders in 2024, when rumour spread online while police said little. The law has not changed.L20

Politicians too

The law covers everyone who publishes, including ministers and people on social media. Before the Southport trial in January 2025, the Attorney General warned social media users, as well as editors, not to assume guilt.L22

5 · Why it matters

Why this base, and why now

RAF Fairford is, in the words of the Guardian’s defence editor, “essentially an American base on British soil”: the European home of the US Air Force’s bombers.18

That matters because of the war the US began against Iran in the spring. Here is how the base came into it.

March

Bombers arrive

Four US B-1 Lancer bombers land at Fairford after Keir Starmer, then prime minister, allows the US to use British bases for what the Ministry of Defence calls “specific defensive operations” against Iranian missile sites.11

On the record

23 July

Iran names the base

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards say any base used to attack Iran is “a legitimate target”, and name Fairford’s role. It comes on the 13th straight day of US bombing.118

On the record

Late Sept

Highest alert

The base had been at its highest threat level for several days before the arrests. British sources say this reflected a general worry about US bases, not a warning about this site.18

Reported

The pattern police are alert to

Laurence Taylor, head of counter-terrorism policing, said on Monday that threats from foreign states often show up as “criminal activity, often at a small scale”, carried out by people whose main aim is “a quick financial gain” rather than terrorism.3 He was describing the wider threat, not saying what these five men did. The Guardian notes that Iran is suspected of using such proxies in earlier cases in Britain.3

The base has also drawn anti-war protests since the bombing began.8 Police have not linked the arrests to any protest group.

6 · Our own rules

What we left out, and why

This page follows the law it explains. We have not:

  • named the men, or described how they looked. Police have not named them, and naming people before charge carries legal risk.L7
  • used photos of the arrests. AI-sharpened versions of one blurred photo spread online over the weekend, and fact-checkers found them fake; they were used to make false claims about the men’s backgrounds.1617
  • repeated single-outlet reports about what was in the vans, or witness accounts of what the men did. That is evidence a jury may one day weigh.

We have reported what politicians claimed, because it is news in its own right, but we have labelled it as claim and set it next to what police have said.

About this page

How it was made

I asked Claude Code for this page at about 4pm on Tuesday 29 September 2026, as a worked example for the AI working group. The test: can an AI agent, in about an hour, turn a fast, messy, legally sensitive story into something clear, sourced and safe to read?

Claude sent three research agents out at once. One built the timeline from police statements and news reports. One gathered what politicians and others had claimed, and what fact-checkers had found. One read the law itself: the Contempt of Court Act, the Police and Criminal Evidence Act, the Terrorism Acts and the key court judgments. They drew on the Guardian’s own reporting through its Content API, on wires and broadcasters, and on legislation.gov.uk. Claude then checked the main facts against the two central Guardian reports by hand, and built the page.

The maps use Natural Earth coastlines and OpenStreetMap data. The page is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. It works without JavaScript, by keyboard and with a screen reader, and it stills its motion for people who ask their device to reduce it.

What to be wary of

No lawyer has read this, and no editor has signed it off. The legal section rests on the text of the law, but applying it to a live case is a lawyer’s job. It is a snapshot, not a live page, and the story will move. If you spot an error, tell me.
