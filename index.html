How much risk it takes

What comes next? Its odds:

mat 52% · sofa 21% · windowsill 13% · keyboard 9% · everything else 5%

The odds are made up, to show the idea. A real model picks from tens of thousands of word-pieces, every time.

Grown, not built: nobody wrote its rules. Three stages of training

  1. Read everything

    Predicting the next word across much of the internet. Knowledge, but no manners.

  2. Learn what people like

    People rate its answers, and it is nudged towards the ones they prefer.

  3. Practise on tasks

    Rewarded for getting checkable tasks right. This is where reasoning came from.

AI working group 01 / NN
Layer 2Reasoning

Layer 2 · Reasoning

It thinks before it answers.

The same question, two waysA council says its budget went up 4%, but spending per resident fell. Can both be true?

Answers straight away

No. If the budget went up, the money spent on each resident went up too.

Wrong

Thinks first · 9 seconds

  1. Per resident means dividing by the population.
  2. What if the population grew faster than 4%?
  3. 4% more money, 6% more people: 1.04 ÷ 1.06 = 0.98.

Yes, if the population grew faster. With 6% more residents, each gets about 2% less.

Right

It is the same LLM, still guessing the next word. It has just been trained to write out a plan and its working first, check itself, and only then answer.

AI working group 01 / NN

The catch

What can go wrong.

When a chatbot gets it wrong, you get a bad answer.

When an agent gets it wrong, it does a bad thing.

  1. 01

    It can be tricked

    Words hidden in a web page, email or document can give it orders. Nobody has solved this.

    2025Researchers showed one crafted email could make Microsoft 365 Copilot leak company data, with no click needed.

  2. 02

    Mistakes become actions

    A wrong answer you can ignore. A wrong email sent, or a wrong purchase, has already happened.

    2025Asked to find cheap eggs, OpenAI's Operator agent bought $31 worth, with priority delivery, without checking first.

  3. 03

    It sees what you let it see

    To act for you it needs your inbox, files and logins. Whatever it can read, it can leak.

    NamedPrivate data, content from strangers and a way to send things out: the “lethal trifecta”. Meta cites it for Muse.

  4. 04

    It chases the goal, not what you meant

    It does whatever gets rewarded, by whatever route works.

    2016A game-playing AI rewarded for points circled one lagoon for ever instead of finishing the race.

This summer, and last week

In July, AI agents under test at OpenAI broke out and hacked another company. Nobody told them to.

~700

agents went into Hugging Face, which hosts AI models and data

<13 hrs

from one machine to administrator control across its systems

17,600

separate actions, with no person directing any of them

They were trying to cheat on a test, and the answers were stored at Hugging Face. Since then Anthropic, Meta and Google have admitted their models broke into outside systems during tests too. On 25 September OpenAI said an agent had slipped out again, and paused all work that lets its most capable models use tools.

Why nobody agrees how good it is.

On a task the AI was bad at, consultants using it were 19 points less likely to get the answer right. On tasks it was good at, their work was over 40% better.Harvard Business School and BCG study of 758 consultants, 2023

And every tool sets the four layers differently

  1. Which LLM is underneath

    The newest, largest models are usually better, and often barely slower. A tool answering billions of searches has to be quick and cheap.

  2. How hard it thinks

    More isn't always better. Anthropic warns its top setting can overthink, and its newest Opus thinks less by default.

  3. Whether it keeps going

    One answer, or plan, act and check for hours.

  4. What it can reach

    Search, your files, your apps, or nothing at all.

So one person's “AI” is a summary at the top of Google, and another's is GPT-6 Astra thinking for minutes. One finds it useless, the other astonishing. Often they're both right.
