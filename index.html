Layer 1 · The LLM

How much risk it takes

What comes next? Its odds:

mat 52% · sofa 21% · windowsill 13% · keyboard 9% · everything else 5%

The odds are made up, to show the idea. A real model picks from tens of thousands of word-pieces, every time.

Grown, not built: nobody wrote its rules. Three stages of training

  1. Read everything

    Predicting the next word across much of the internet. Knowledge, but no manners.

  2. Learn what people like

    People rate its answers, and it is nudged towards the ones they prefer.

  3. Practise on tasks

    Rewarded for getting checkable tasks right. This is where reasoning came from.

AI working group 01 / NN
Layer 2Reasoning

Layer 2 · Reasoning

It thinks before it answers.

The same question, two waysA council says its budget went up 4%, but spending per resident fell. Can both be true?

Answers straight away

No. If the budget went up, the money spent on each resident went up too.

Wrong

Thinks first

  1. Per resident means dividing by the population.
  2. What if the population grew faster than 4%?
  3. 4% more money, 6% more people: 1.04 ÷ 1.06 = 0.98.

Yes, if the population grew faster. With 6% more residents, each gets about 2% less.

Right

It is the same LLM, still guessing the next word. It has just been trained to write out a plan and its working first, check itself, and only then answer.

AI working group 01 / NN

The catch

What can go wrong.

When a chatbot gets it wrong, you get a bad answer.

When an agent gets it wrong, it does a bad thing.

  1. 01

    It can be tricked

    Words hidden in a web page, email or document can give it orders. Nobody has solved this.

    2025Researchers showed one crafted email could make Microsoft 365 Copilot leak company data, with no click needed.

  2. 02

    Mistakes become actions

    A wrong answer you can ignore. A wrong email sent, or a wrong purchase, has already happened.

    2025Asked to find cheap eggs, OpenAI's Operator agent bought $31 worth, with priority delivery, without checking first.

  3. 03

    It sees what you let it see

    To act for you it needs your inbox, files and logins. Whatever it can read, it can leak.

    NamedPrivate data, content from strangers and a way to send things out: the “lethal trifecta”. Meta cites it for Muse.

  4. 04

    It chases the goal, not what you meant

    It does whatever gets rewarded, by whatever route works.

    2016A game-playing AI rewarded for points circled one lagoon for ever instead of finishing the race.

So where does that leave us

Why nobody agrees how good it is.

Its ability is jagged · an illustration

−19

On a task the AI was bad at, consultants using it were 19 points less likely to get the answer right. On tasks it was good at, their work was over 40% better.Harvard Business School and BCG study of 758 consultants, 2023

And every tool sets the four layers differently

  1. Which LLM is underneath

    The newest, largest models are usually better, and often barely slower. A tool answering billions of searches has to be quick and cheap.

  2. How hard it thinks

    More isn't always better. Anthropic warns its top setting can overthink, and its newest Opus thinks less by default.

  3. Whether it keeps going

    One answer, or plan, act and check for hours.

  4. What it can reach

    Search, your files, your apps, or nothing at all.

So one person's “AI” is a summary at the top of Google, and another's is GPT-6 Astra thinking for minutes. One finds it useless, the other astonishing. Often they're both right.

What makes a good one

  • Broad enough to dig into. More than one question, more than one source.
  • Contested, or still moving. Where rumour, spin or strong opinion can creep in.

Best of all, pick something from your own patch. You'll know within a minute whether the answer is any good.

Read it like an editor… and a reader.

Can we trust it?

  • Is it right, and how would you know?
  • Which sources did it choose, and which did it miss?
  • Whose framing is it borrowing?

How does it compare with us?

  • What would our version have that this lacks?
  • What does it do that we can't, or don't?
  • Would a reader notice the difference?

  • Who would reach for this instead of us, and why?

How accurate are they?

True enough?

  1. 91%
    AI Overviews

    right on 4,326 hard factual questions, up from 85% before Google moved it to Gemini 3.

    Oumi for the New York Times, October 2025 and February 2026. Google disputes the test set.

  2. 56%
    AI Overviews

    of those right answers linked to sources that didn't fully back them up.

    Same study. Google has never published an error rate.

  3. 89%
    AI Overviews

    of 98,000 claims from trending US searches matched the pages cited. 2.7% were contradicted by them.

    Washington University in St Louis, spring 2026, not yet peer reviewed.

  4. 45%
    ChatGPT · Copilot · Gemini · Perplexity

    of news answers had a significant problem, mostly sourcing. Gemini 76%, Copilot 37%, ChatGPT 36%, Perplexity 30%.

    22 public broadcasters led by the EBU and BBC, free versions, May and June 2025.

Why they go wrong

  • Making it up. Pure invention: rarer now, and likeliest where little has been written.
  • Answering too fast. No time to think, so no chance to check.
  • Trusting the wrong source. Satire, a forum joke or one weak page, taken at face value.
  • Missing the right source. Blocked, paywalled, or too new to reach.

Some famous slips

  • Told pancreatic cancer patients to avoid high-fat foods, the opposite of expert advice. AI Overviews · Guardian investigation, January 2026
  • Said the Air India plane that crashed in June 2025 was an Airbus. It was a Boeing. AI Overviews
  • Suggested glue to keep cheese on pizza, from an old forum joke. AI Overviews · May 2024

Meanwhile, in the background

The far end of the scale.

While you worked, Claude ran as an agent in its own harness, on the same story. You don't have it here; this is what the other end of part one looks like.

The brief it was given

Build a sourced interactive explainer on today's story: what is confirmed, what is reported, what is only claimed, and why so much is unclear. Link every claim to its source, then check each source says what you say it says.

  • How it works

    All four layers: it plans, searches, reads whole pages, writes the page, checks its own work and goes round again.

  • What it can reach

    Its own web search, plus the pages it opens. Publishers can block it too.

  • Watch for

    The same as the rest: it's only as good as what it can read, and it still needs an editor.

  • How long

    As long as the job takes.
