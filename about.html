About this deck

AI working group, session one.

The opening session for ten senior editors: where AI has got to, how it got there, and what that means for the news. This page says what the deck is, how it was made, and what changed and why.

What this is

Slides for the first of four one-hour sessions of the ELG working group on AI, agents and the future of our journalism. Part one is a twenty-minute overview of where the technology stands. Part two puts different AI tools to work on the same news story.

Part one has a single idea, built up one layer at a time: a language model that guesses the next word; reasoning, which lets it work a problem through first; the loop, which lets it keep going until a job is done; and the harness, the tools and access built around it. Put together, that is an agent. Each layer keeps one colour throughout, so the diagram on the map slide can be read again on every slide that follows.

  • Every figure has a source. They are listed in SOURCES.md alongside the deck, each labelled by how it was checked.
  • Arrow keys or space to move, R to reset, G to show the grid, Cmd-P to save a PDF.

How it's made

Plain HTML and CSS, no build step. It is built from the Guardian deck template, second edition, by way of the vibe coding workshop deck, which carries the board training components too. The fonts load from Guardian servers, so it wants a network connection to look right.

I built it by describing it to Claude Code and letting it do the typing. The research behind the figures was done the same way, from primary sources where they exist, and anything that could not be checked has been left out.

Diary

Milestone30 September 2026

Part two: one story, five tools

The hands-on half took shape over a long evening and an early morning. The room mostly thinks AI Overviews are terrible and stand for AI in general, so the aim was to show five tools built for five different jobs: Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode, Gemini with Canvas, Gemini Notebook (NotebookLM until Google renamed it in July) and ChatGPT thinking hard. Each gets a slide with a prompt to copy and a plain account of what it's for and what it can reach.

Two things I got wrong on the way. A map that set reach against thinking looked neat and wasn't true, so it became a chart of what each tool is built for. And a slide on AI Overviews' accuracy turned out to be about all of them, so it moved ahead of the tools as "True enough?", with every figure tagged by the tool it measured. The widely quoted 76% was the Gemini app, not AI Overviews, which I suspect will surprise people.

The first half gained a slide it was missing: that the models themselves got far better, shown by three tests built to be hard and passed within two years. It ends on the ask: take them to work, on the difficult things, and come back with a brief.

Change29 September 2026, evening

A race on the reasoning slide

The reasoning slide showed two finished answers side by side, which told you the difference but didn't let you feel it. Now they stream out the way a model writes, word by word. The one that answers straight away is done, and wrong, in about a second. The one that thinks first sits there "thinking" with a count ticking up while its working appears, and only then writes its answer. The chart of OpenAI's own numbers waits until the race is over. It's the same trick as the typed prompts in the other decks, and I think it does more than any caption could.

Change29 September 2026, evening

The word toy comes over from the field guide

The static picture of next-word odds never did the job as well as the little toy in my AI risk field guide, where you pick a word or let the model roll the dice. So it came across, restyled for the deck. Choose "keyboard" and the odds for the next word change; set it to wild and the same start ends differently. That second point sets up the last slide rather nicely. The one snag in a deck is the space bar, which turns the slide, so the toy's buttons keep it for themselves.

Change29 September 2026, later

One idea, and a proper polish

Looking at the first build, I wanted less. Part one has one job: to show that this is no longer just a language model, and to pull apart the layers that got us from a chatbot to agents that are genuinely useful, and to Hugging Face. So the news and search-traffic figures came out, along with the close about what it all means for news. They belong in session four, which is about the business.

The new last slide says something I keep running into: nobody agrees how good this stuff is. Partly because its ability is jagged, brilliant at olympiad maths and hopeless at admitting it doesn't know. Partly because "AI" covers everything from Google's one-second search summary to an agent that works for hours. A Harvard and BCG study of 758 consultants puts a number on the danger: on a task the AI was bad at, the ones using it were 19 points less likely to get it right.

Then a design pass, because too many slides were unbalanced or flat. The reasoning slide was the one I cared about most. It now asks the same question two ways, one answer wrong and one right, next to OpenAI's own figures: 12% against 74% on the same maths test. The agent's sixteen hours, which I couldn't parse myself, is now a small chart that goes from three and a half minutes to sixteen hours in three years.

Milestone29 September 2026

Part one: four layers, and a lot of checking

The brief to myself was to take the "current landscape" section of the board training deck, make it clearer and make it land harder. The board version had the right idea in three cards: a reasoning brain, a harness, an agentic loop. What it didn't have was an order. So this version builds the thing up one layer at a time, starting with the model itself, and gives each layer a colour it keeps for the rest of the deck. The ring diagram on the map slide shows them nested, which is the point: each one was built around the one before.

A lot of the explanation comes from the AI risk field guide I finished last week, which already had the plainest account of training I've managed: grown, not built, in three stages. It also had the Hugging Face story, which now closes the risks section.

The usage figures got the hardest look, and it was humbling. The board deck compared generative AI at work with the internet and smartphones at the same point after launch. The internet figure turned out to measure all use, not use at work, and I could find no source at all for the smartphone one. The chart is gone. The one comparison I can stand behind is narrower: at work in the US, AI reached the level of use the PC did in a third of the time.

The UK news figures tell a more interesting story than I expected. Only 4% of UK adults use a chatbot for news each week, the lowest of 48 countries, and 6% trust the answers. But 21% came across news through AI in the past month. They aren't choosing it; it's reaching them through search.

Then the news overtook the draft. Meta launched Muse on 8 September, ChatGPT Voice started using people's apps on 23 September, and on 25 September OpenAI paused tool use on its most capable models after an agent got out of a test again. All three are in.
